### [1] [Return or Revise? Learning When Revision Helps Retrieval-Augmented QA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30087)
*Nicholas Kashani Motlagh,Tim Anderson,Jeremy Gwinnup,Grant Erdmann*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: The paper studies how to decide whether to keep a model’s current draft answer or to revise it using retrieved evidence, proposing a new notion called recoverability and showing it predicts when revision helps better than simple confidence scores.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: In answer-revision and RAG-style systems, models first produce a draft answer and then may revise it using retrieved documents. Existing decisions about whether to revise usually rely on draft confidence—how likely the current answer is correct. However, being confident in the draft does not directly indicate whether revision will improve or harm the answer. Some revisions can repair wrong drafts, but others can make correct drafts worse. The authors want a way to estimate the *effect* of a specific revision option, not just the correctness of the existing answer, so that systems can more intelligently choose among returning the draft, revising it, or using other alternatives like a standard RAG answer.

Method: The authors introduce “recoverability,” defined as the paired effect between a draft answer and its candidate revision. For offline training and evaluation, they grade both the draft and its revision with the same correctness judge, making it possible to observe when revision repairs errors, causes harm, or how far performance is from an oracle policy that always chooses the better option. They train policies (scorers) to predict this recoverability *before* the actual revision is applied, enabling the system at inference time to choose whether to revise. They compare this recoverability-based scorer with a matched draft-correctness scorer across 25,870 held-out open-domain questions, three revision setups, and multiple Llama configurations and seeds. They also test scenarios where an additional draft-free standard RAG answer is available and the system must choose between draft, revision, and RAG output.

Result: Across three revision setups and nine Llama configuration–seed combinations, the recoverability-based scorer achieves higher area under the accuracy–revision-rate curve than an otherwise similar scorer based purely on draft correctness. At development-selected operating thresholds, recoverability-based policies gain 0.23–0.68 accuracy points on average over draft-correctness policies. These gains are statistically significant across training runs for dense retrieval scenarios. Policies based on recoverability outperform a naive strategy of always revising and close more than one-third of the oracle performance gap, though they still accept 38–46% of harmful revisions. When the system can also choose a separate standard-RAG answer (generated without using the draft), simply selecting between draft and RAG answers yields about 2 accuracy points improvement for Llama and 4 for OLMo over draft-only setups, and adding the candidate revision as a third choice gives no significant further gains.

Conclusion: Recoverability is a useful notion for predicting the benefit of a specific answer revision, improving decision policies over those relying only on draft confidence and outperforming naive always-revise strategies. It closes a substantial portion of the gap to an oracle revision selector but still allows many harmful revisions, indicating room for better estimation. However, when a high-quality standard-RAG answer is available as an alternative, the value of using answer revision diminishes: simply choosing between the draft and the RAG answer often outperforms strategies that also consider a candidate revision. Thus, recoverability effectively characterizes the utility of a single revision action, but its practical value depends strongly on what other answer-generation options are present in the system.

Abstract: We consider the decision of whether to return an existing draft answer or revise it using retrieved evidence, as in answer-revision systems. Draft confidence estimates whether the current answer is correct, but the decision requires estimating the effect of a specified revision. For offline training and evaluation, we grade both the returned draft and its candidate revision under the same correctness judge, which makes repair, harm, and the gap to an oracle observable. We call this paired effect its recoverability, and we train policies to predict it before revision. On 25,870 held-out open-domain questions across three revision setups, a scorer trained on the paired outcome has greater area under the accuracy--revision-rate curve than a matched draft-correctness scorer in all nine Llama setup--seed fits, and gains 0.23--0.68 accuracy points on average at development-selected thresholds, a difference significant across training runs only for dense retrieval. The resulting policy improves on always revising and on average closes more than a third of the oracle gap, although it still applies 38--46% of the harmful revisions. When a draft-free standard-RAG answer is also available, however, choosing between the draft and that answer is stronger by about two points for Llama and four for OLMo, and adding candidate revision as a third option yields no significant gain. Recoverability describes one revision; its value as an available action also depends on the alternatives.

  <summary>Details</summary>

### [3] [Does a model's stated reason for rejecting a candidate do any work?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30151)
*Archit Rastogi*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: The paper studies how language models change their candidate choices when missing facts are added to rival profiles, and disentangles genuine content effects from artifacts of where and how sentences are inserted.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Language models, when asked to choose between candidate answers and explain their choice, often justify rejecting a rival by pointing out that some key fact is missing from its profile. This behavior looks like genuine reasoning about factual content, but it might instead reflect sensitivity to superficial factors such as sentence position or particular wordings. The authors want a way to test, using only model inputs and outputs (no human judges), whether supplying the supposedly missing fact actually changes the model’s preferences as claimed, and to separate the role of content from other confounds.

Method: The authors design a controlled intervention on 2WikiMultihopQA using six open models. For each question where a model prefers one candidate over a rival and cites a missing fact in its explanation (e.g., no director/date of death is given), they construct several modified input conditions under greedy decoding: (1) a “repair” condition where they insert a true corpus sentence that states the named missing fact into the rival’s profile; (2) an “irrelevant control” where they insert a length-matched sentence that is content-irrelevant but placed at the same rival profile location; and (3) parallel insertions of both sentences into a third option that the model never mentioned. They then re-query the model and measure how often its choice flips. They further examine secondary properties (co-candidate mentions, relation template similarities, fluency) and run post-hoc matching to see how much these confounds account for the observed effects. They also compare these intervention effects on free-text choices to a forced single-token probability reading, and use string-based rules for measurement, validating these rules against saved records to detect defects.

Result: In the largest run, adding the named missing fact to the rival’s profile changes model choices significantly more than adding the irrelevant control sentence at the same place (odds ratio 3.57 with 95% CI [1.54, 8.26], Holm-corrected p=0.0210), and this effect is robust to dropping any single model. In contrast, inserting the same fact at a third, previously unmentioned option does not produce a statistically reliable effect after multiple-comparisons correction (Holm p=0.2428). The strongest statistical result concerns placement alone: even the irrelevant sentence, when placed at the rival the model had named, shifts choices more than when placed at the third option (Holm p=0.0008). Post-hoc matching on co-candidate mentions and relation template still leaves the content-effect direction unchanged, while matching on fluency weakens one content contrast, suggesting the estimated content effects likely upper-bound the true effect. A separate analysis of single-token probabilities yields an effect direction opposite to that seen in free-text choices, and three investigated explanations for this discrepancy are not supported by the data. Validation of the string-based measurement rules uncovers eight defects, including a major choice-parsing bug that would have misattributed rejected options in 17.1% of adjudicable responses and inflated the number of surviving contrasts from four to six.

Conclusion: The study finds evidence that supplying a fact which a language model itself cited as missing can indeed influence its subsequent choices, but that this effect is intertwined with, and often smaller than, strong placement and formatting effects. Content interventions on the rival the model names matter more than equivalent interventions on a third, unmentioned option, yet even content-irrelevant changes at the named rival can significantly sway decisions. Differences between free-text choices and single-token probability measurements caution against interpreting token-level probabilities as a straightforward proxy for decision behavior. Finally, careful validation of automated string-based analysis pipelines is crucial, as seemingly minor parsing errors can materially distort conclusions.

Abstract: Asked to choose between candidates and explain the choice, a language model often rejects a rival by naming a fact its profile lacks: no director, no date of death. That sentence is a claim about the text in front of the model, and it can be tested without any judge. We insert a real corpus sentence stating the named fact into the rival's profile and ask again under greedy decoding. Two controls separate content from placement: a length-matched irrelevant sentence at the same profile, and the same two sentences at a third option the model never mentioned. In the largest of three runs, six open models on 2WikiMultihopQA, supplying the named fact at the profile the model named moves its choice more than the irrelevant control does, odds ratio 3.57 [1.54, 8.26], Holm p=0.0210, and this survives dropping any single model. The contrast the design was built to detect, the same fact at the option nobody named, does not clear correction, Holm p=0.2428. The strongest result in the family carries no content claim at all: the identical irrelevant sentence moves the choice more at the named rival than at the third option, Holm p=0.0008. Repair and control also differ in co-candidate mentions, relation template and fluency; post-hoc matching on the first two preserves the content effects' direction, matching fluency weakens one, so the content contrasts bound an effect rather than establish one. A forced single-token probability read disagrees in direction with the free-text choice on that same contrast, and three candidate explanations for the disagreement find no support. Every measurement is a string rule, so each was validated against the records it reads; validation caught eight defects. The largest, a choice-parsing rule that returned the option a model had just rejected in 17.1% of adjudicable responses, would have reported six surviving contrasts instead of four.

### [4] [Do Audio Language Models Hear and Read Distinctive Features Alike?](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.30167)
*Yuanhao Chen,Peter Chin*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: The paper probes audio language models to see whether they encode the same phonological features (like voicing) in a shared direction for both heard and written phonemes, finding consistent cross-modal representation mainly for voicing in specific model families.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Audio language models process both speech and text with a single decoder, but it is unclear whether the internal representations for phonological features are shared across modalities. The authors want to know if the same abstract feature (e.g., voicing) is encoded in a consistent direction in representation space when a sound is heard versus when its written form is read, and how this varies across models, features, and languages.

Method: For each model, the authors collect minimal phoneme pairs that differ in a single phonological feature across multiple languages. They compute mean internal representations for each phoneme, take the vector offset between phonemes in a minimal pair, and average these offsets to obtain a feature direction separately for audio and text streams. They then compute the cosine similarity between the two modality-specific feature directions. To control for the fact that phoneme pairs already show non-zero similarity, they construct a reference distribution using random pairings of phonemes and compare their measurements against this reference instead of zero. The analysis is performed on 6 models, 7 phonological features, and 15 languages from 11 language families, with multiple-testing correction applied.

Result: Across models, only the voicing feature in the two Qwen2.5-Omni models shows cross-modal feature directions that significantly exceed the random-pairing reference after correcting for multiple comparisons. The strength of the reference distribution differs substantially between models, varying by up to a factor of seven. In three of the six models, the audio stream alone exhibits a single, consistent voicing direction across the 14 languages with sufficient minimal pairs; in two of these models, all language pairs agree on this direction.

Conclusion: The internal encoding of phonological features in audio language models is not uniformly shared between audio and text streams. Robust, modality-aligned feature directions are rare and are observed mainly for voicing, particularly in specific model families such as Qwen2.5-Omni. Cross-linguistic consistency of feature directions for voicing appears in the audio stream for some models, and the likelihood that a feature is explicitly represented depends more on the model family than on model size.

Abstract: Audio language models pass speech and text through a single decoder. We ask whether that decoder represents a distinctive feature in the same direction when a phoneme is heard and when it is read. For minimal pairs of phonemes differing in one feature, we take the offset between the two members' mean representations. Averaging those offsets gives a direction for each stream, and we measure the cosine between the two. Because the two streams already agree about arbitrary phoneme pairs, we compare every measure against a reference built from random pairings rather than against zero. We apply this to 6 models, 7 features and 15 languages from 11 families. Only voicing in the two Qwen2.5-Omni models exceeds that reference after correction for multiple testing, and the reference varies by a factor of seven between models. In three of the six models, voicing has one direction in audio across the 14 languages with enough minimal pairs to measure it, and every language pair agrees in two of them. The model family, not the model size, predicts which stream represents a feature.
