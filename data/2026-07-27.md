### [1] [Adversarial Style Optimization: Enhancing VLM Jailbreaks by GRPO-based Stylistic Triggers Optimization](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21619)
*Bingjun Luo,Jialin Guo,Yue Yao,Xinpeng Ding*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: The paper reveals and exploits a stylistic inconsistency in multimodal large language models (MLLMs) to dramatically strengthen visual jailbreak attacks via an adversarial style optimization module.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Although MLLMs show strong multimodal capabilities, their safety alignment is fragile and can be bypassed by jailbreak attacks. Existing visual/content-based jailbreak methods often perform inconsistently across different models and do not effectively exploit non-content factors, such as style, that might influence safety filters. The authors are motivated to systematically explore whether visual style itself can act as a reliable attack vector and how to algorithmically optimize such stylistic perturbations to boost jailbreak success against evolving MLLMs.

Method: The authors first empirically study a "Stylistic Inconsistency" in MLLMs: models can consistently understand content across diverse visual styles, but their safety mechanisms are sensitive to certain styles, which can serve as triggers to bypass defenses. Building on this, they introduce Adversarial Style Optimization (ASO), a plug-and-play enhancement module that takes an existing adversarial image and learns a style overlay to intensify its jailbreak effect. ASO fine-tunes an image-editing model to apply an optimized stylistic modification, guided by a reinforcement-learning framework based on Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO). The optimization uses a Structurally-Tiered Reward Function that combines: (1) a logit-based signal indicating whether the target MLLM is refusing the request, and (2) a high-fidelity semantic evaluation from a stronger judge model to ensure that the adversarial intent is preserved. This closed-loop optimization iteratively improves the style pattern to maximize attack success while retaining the original semantic content.

Result: Experiments on state-of-the-art MLLMs demonstrate that ASO consistently and significantly raises the attack success rate (ASR) of multiple existing visual jailbreak methods. The results validate that stylistic biases in MLLM safety mechanisms are real, exploitable, and transfer across models. The method works as a plug-and-play booster, amplifying SOTA attacks without needing to redesign their core content, and shows scalability as a red-teaming tool for evaluating and stress-testing safety alignment in MLLMs.

Conclusion: The paper concludes that MLLMs harbor a systemic stylistic inconsistency: they can understand visual content robustly across styles, yet their safety filters can be reliably subverted by optimized stylistic cues. Adversarial Style Optimization (ASO) operationalizes this insight by learning style overlays that markedly increase jailbreak success rates when combined with existing attacks. This reveals stylistic bias as a powerful, scalable red-teaming vector, highlighting an important and underexplored dimension of safety alignment that future MLLM defenses must address.

Abstract: Multimodal Large Language Models (MLLMs) have achieved impressive performance, but their safety alignment remains vulnerable to jailbreak attacks. Existing content-based jailbreaks are often inconsistent and show unsatisfying performance against the rapidly evolving MLLMs, failing to exploit non-content-based vulnerabilities. Unlike previous research, we empirically find that MLLMs exhibit a Stylistic Inconsistency between their comprehension ability and safety ability: MLLMs can robustly understand content regardless of visual style, yet their defense mechanisms can be easily bypassed by specific stylistic triggers. Based on this finding, we propose Adversarial Style Optimization (ASO), a plug-and-play enhancement module to amplify existing visual jailbreaks. ASO fine-tunes an image-editing model to superimpose an optimized stylistic modification onto a given adversarial image, using a Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) agent guided by a Structurally-Tiered Reward Function that combines a logit-based signal for detecting explicit refusals with a high-fidelity semantic evaluation from a powerful judge model. Extensive experiments show that ASO significantly enhances the ASR of SOTA attacks, demonstrating that stylistic biases are a scalable vector for red-teaming MLLMs. Our code is available at https://github.com/bingjunluo/ASO.

### [2] [A Consensus-Based Framework for Relative Preference Evaluation of Large Language Models](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.21632)
*Mohtashim Khan*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: The paper proposes a consensus-based, LLM-driven evaluation framework that ranks model responses by inter-model preferences instead of static ground-truth correctness, yielding a Relative Intelligence Index across domains.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Existing LLM benchmarks typically depend on static datasets and objective, correctness-based metrics. These fail in open-ended or multi-answer settings where many responses can be valid but differ in clarity, completeness, and usefulness. The authors aim to develop an evaluation method that better captures nuanced response quality when correctness alone is not discriminative.

Method: The authors design a consensus-based evaluation framework where multiple state-of-the-art LLMs act as both answer generators and evaluators. For each prompt across several domains (programming, general knowledge, safety, logical reasoning, mathematics), each model generates a response. Then, a panel of diverse LLMs independently ranks anonymized candidate responses to that same prompt via a structured voting process. The inter-model rankings are aggregated into a Relative Intelligence Index (RII) that quantifies how often each model’s responses are preferred by others, under blind conditions without reference to a fixed ground truth.

Result: The study using five state-of-the-art LLMs shows stable and consistent preference patterns across all tested domains: some models’ responses are more frequently preferred by their peers, thus achieving higher RII scores. This indicates non-random, systematic differences in how models perceive the relative quality of each other’s outputs. The authors also note that the framework captures inter-model preference alignment rather than strict correctness or human-labeled performance.

Conclusion: The paper concludes that a consensus-based, inter-model preference framework is a scalable and informative alternative to traditional, correctness-only benchmarks, particularly in tasks with multiple acceptable answers. The Relative Intelligence Index offers a comparative signal of response quality as perceived by other models. While it does not replace human evaluation and is not a direct measure of objective correctness, prior work showing partial correlation between model preferences and human judgments suggests it can serve as a useful proxy signal for evaluating and comparing LLMs.

Abstract: Traditional benchmarks for LLMs primarily rely on static datasets and objective scoring metrics, which often fail to capture differences in response quality when multiple answers are acceptable. In such settings, correctness alone is insufficient to distinguish between responses that vary in clarity, completeness, and usefulness.
  This paper introduces a consensus-based evaluation framework that measures relative preference among model-generated responses rather than absolute correctness. Instead of evaluating outputs against a fixed ground truth, we assess how a panel of diverse LLMs ranks anonymized candidate responses to the same prompt. This approach treats aggregate inter-model agreement as a proxy for perceived response quality under blind conditions.
  We conduct a controlled study using five state-of-the-art LLMs across multiple domains, including programming, general knowledge, safety, logical reasoning, and mathematics. Each model generates responses and independently ranks peer outputs through a structured voting process. Scores are aggregated into a Relative Intelligence Index (RII), representing how frequently a model's responses are preferred by other models.
  Our findings reveal consistent preference patterns across domains, with certain models more frequently ranked highly by their peers. However, we emphasize that these results reflect inter-model preference alignment rather than objective correctness or human judgment. This framework provides a scalable, model-driven method for comparative evaluation, offering an alternative perspective on response quality in scenarios where multiple valid answers exist. While not directly aligned with human evaluation, prior work suggests that aggregated model preferences can partially correlate with human judgments, motivating this as a proxy signal.

TL;DR: The paper compares expert-assigned vs automatically generated MeSH terms as features for document classifiers used in systematic review screening, showing that their relative benefit is small and highly sensitive to evaluation design.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>

These classifiers often use MeSH terms as additional features, which can come from expert human indexers (delayed but presumably high-quality) or automatic tools (immediate but noisier). Despite their widespread use, there has been no direct, controlled comparison of expert vs automatic MeSH as classifier features, nor an investigation of how conclusions about their utility depend on experimental design choices such as corpus size and cross-validation scheme. The authors aim to fill this gap and to highlight how methodological choices can change benchmark-driven conclusions about feature quality.

Method: Using the Cohen et al. (2006) drug-class benchmark, the authors focus on three topics (Statins, Opioids, ADHD). They evaluate two classifiers: (1) a bag-of-words logistic regression model (run seven times) and (2) a BiomedBERT transformer model (run with five random seeds). Both are tested with either expert-assigned MeSH terms or automatically generated MeSH terms as additional features. Performance is mainly measured by WSS@95% (work saved over sampling at 95% recall). The authors systematically vary evaluation design: canonical 5-fold cross-validation on the full corpus, reduced corpus size matched to smaller topics, and 10-fold cross-validation at full size. They also conduct a power analysis to determine whether non-significant results on some topics are due to insufficient statistical power. Finally, they analyse representation constraints in BiomedBERT (512-token limit) when long inputs arise from appending expert MeSH terms.

Result: On the Statins topic under canonical 5-fold cross-validation with the full corpus, the bag-of-words classifier shows a +0.096 WSS@95% advantage for expert vs automatic MeSH features. When the corpus size is reduced to match the smaller topics (n = 803), this gap shrinks to +0.033 with a 95% bootstrap confidence interval that includes zero. Using 10-fold cross-validation at the full Statins corpus size further reduces the gap to +0.021, with the CI just excluding zero. BiomedBERT, evaluated canonically, yields a +0.020 gap, statistically indistinguishable from the bag-of-words 10-fold result. Power analysis reveals that an effect of the size observed for Statins would not be detectable given the higher variance for Opioids and ADHD, implying that their null findings are driven by design and variance limitations, not necessarily true absence of effect. Additionally, 15.1% of Statins inputs exceed BiomedBERT’s 512-token limit when expert MeSH terms are added, forcing truncation that may dampen any benefit from expert MeSH. Across transformers and 10-fold bag-of-words setups, the observed expert-vs-auto MeSH gap on tested topics is roughly 0.02 WSS@95%, with confidence intervals that touch or cross zero.

Conclusion: The advantage of expert-assigned over automatically generated MeSH terms as classifier features in systematic review screening is small (around 0.02 WSS@95%) and highly sensitive to evaluation design choices such as corpus size and cross-validation scheme. Under some plausible designs the benefit appears notable; under others it shrinks to near-zero or becomes statistically ambiguous. For transformer-based models, input length limits and truncation further complicate the comparison. Consequently, strong benchmark claims about the superiority of one MeSH source over another may be artefacts of specific experimental setups. More generally, the study warns that conclusions about feature sources in text classification benchmarks can change substantially under reasonable modifications to evaluation methodologies, underscoring the need for careful, power-aware experimental design and nuanced interpretation of performance gaps.
