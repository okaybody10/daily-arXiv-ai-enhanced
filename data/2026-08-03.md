### [1] [Can LLMs Really Understand Item Difficulty Levels? Implications for Automated Item Generation Using LLMs](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.28634)
*Xinyi Wang,Hong Jiao,Ming Li,Sydney Peters,Hanna Choi,Tianyi Zhou,Qingshu Xu*

Main category: cs.CL

TL;DR: Study evaluates how well large language models can predict reading/writing test item difficulty and compares them to traditional models.


<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Accurate estimation of item difficulty is central for designing, calibrating, and maintaining both formative and high-stakes assessments. With the rapid rise of LLMs, there is growing interest in whether they can replace or supplement traditional psychometric and NLP methods for predicting item difficulty, especially when human calibration is costly.

Method: The authors used items from a large-scale Reading and Writing test and asked multiple LLMs to predict categorical difficulty levels under various prompting strategies and parameter settings (e.g., temperature, zero-shot setups). They compared LLM performance—focusing on quadratic weighted kappa (QWK)—against encoder-only language models such as ConvBERT and against feature-based supervised machine learning models. They also analyzed error patterns (especially on hard items), examined systematic biases (e.g., underestimation of difficulty by stronger models like GPT-5.4), and conducted dimensionality reduction on item embeddings to inspect how items cluster by difficulty level.

Result: Zero-shot GPT-4.1 with temperature 0 achieved the best LLM performance, with QWK = 0.578 for item difficulty level prediction. However, ConvBERT achieved higher accuracy (QWK = 0.625), surpassing both LLMs and the best feature-based supervised ML model. All LLMs struggled particularly with hard items, and advanced GPT-5.4 systematically underestimated item difficulty. Embedding-based visualization showed that items from different difficulty levels were not well separated in the embedding space, suggesting that semantic content alone does not strongly encode difficulty.

Conclusion: Current LLMs, even advanced ones, do not yet match strong encoder-only models like ConvBERT in predicting item difficulty levels for reading/writing tests. Their systematic tendency to underestimate difficulty and treat most items as easy, coupled with mixed difficulty clusters in embedding space, implies that semantic information alone is insufficient to infer difficulty. Therefore, practitioners should be cautious about relying on LLMs to generate or select items with targeted difficulty levels without additional psychometric calibration or external signals.

Abstract: The estimation of item difficulty plays a key role in both formative assessment and large-scale high-stakes summative assessments. This study explores how large language models (LLMs) perform in predicting item difficulty levels using items from a large-scale Reading and Writing test. The study investigated various prompting strategies and parameter settings across multiple LLMs. LLM performance was compared with encoder-only language models and feature-based supervised machine learning models. Zero-shot GPT-4.1 with a temperature of 0 yielded the highest item difficulty level prediction accuracy, with a quadratic weighted kappa (QWK) of 0.578. However, LLMs' prediction accuracy was lower than that of ConvBERT (QWK = 0.625), which outperformed the best feature-based supervised machine learning model. Further analysis showed that all LLMs struggled to label hard items; in particular, the current advanced GPT-5.4 tended to underestimate item difficulty levels. Dimension reduction of embeddings showed that item embeddings from different difficulty levels were mixed together, indicating that semantic information from items alone is likely insufficient for item difficulty level prediction. The findings suggest that if LLMs cannot understand item difficulty levels as evidenced by empirical data and tend to treat most items as easy when their own capabilities increase, caution should be exercised when using LLMs to generate items with targeted difficulty levels.

<details>
  <summary>Details</summary>
Motivation: Existing bias metrics for language models typically use aggregate stereotype scores, which can hide harmful per-item behaviors such as when a model stops refusing to answer ambiguous, bias-sensitive questions and instead outputs stereotypes. Knowledge distillation is widely used to improve small models by training them on larger teacher models' outputs, but its impact on nuanced bias behaviors like refusal calibration is not well understood. The authors aim to systematically study how distillation affects both context-following and bias, disentangling different failure modes and improving evaluation so these harms are not obscured by average metrics.

Method: The authors perform response-based knowledge distillation from a Gemma-2-9B teacher into smaller instruction-tuned students (SmolLM2-1.7B-Instruct and OLMo-2-1B-Instruct). They evaluate on the BBQ benchmark in both disambiguated (BBQ-disambig) and ambiguous (BBQ-ambig) settings, measuring context-overriding errors, refusal calibration (whether the model abstains where it should), and the emergence of stereotyped answers. They decompose bias changes into two mechanisms: silence-loss (cases where correct refusals are replaced with answers) and filled-silence (extent to which new answers are stereotyped). They analyze correlations across 28 model/distillation configurations. To investigate causes, they audit four training corpora for the presence of refusals as answer-shaped outputs. They experiment with supervised fine-tuning that injects refusals and observe its side effects, including trivial-refuser behavior. Finally, they propose Per-Condition Calibration Diagnosis (PCCD), a three-step evaluation protocol assessing refusal calibration, context-following, and capability preservation separately.

Similar patterns appear for OLMo-2-1B-Instruct, with an 8% silence-loss and 89% of new bias from filled-silence. Across 28 configurations, silence-loss and filled-silence magnitudes are uncorrelated (Spearman ρ=0.19, not significant), suggesting distinct underlying mechanisms. The authors find that common training corpora contain very few instances (<0.5%) where refusals appear in the same shape as typical answers, which they link to the observed calibration loss. SFT with refusal injection either harms parsing or pushes the model into trivial-refuser behavior (≈99.8% refusal rate, ≈0.2% disambig accuracy), which aggregate bias metrics misleadingly rate as perfectly calibrated.

Conclusion: Knowledge distillation in small instruction-tuned LMs has asymmetric effects: it improves context-following on clearly answerable tasks but undermines per-item refusal calibration on ambiguous, bias-sensitive ones by causing both loss of warranted silence and stereotyped filled-silence. These two bias mechanisms are statistically independent and are obscured by aggregate stereotype metrics, which can even falsely endorse trivial-refuser models as well-calibrated. The root cause is partly data-side, as training corpora rarely present refusals in answer-like forms, making it hard for models to learn when and how to abstain appropriately. The authors argue that evaluations should move beyond aggregate scores and adopt Per-Condition Calibration Diagnosis (PCCD), which separately tests refusal calibration, context-following, and capability preservation to reveal asymmetric harms and failure modes introduced by distillation or mitigation strategies.
